About

Who I am, what I build, and why this particular class of bug.

Jorge Aquino

Two careers spent on things that fail quietly.

I was a Marine, and then a Firefighter/EMT. Both jobs teach you the same uncomfortable lesson: the thing that hurts people is rarely the thing making noise. It's the detail nobody checked, the assumption everyone shared, the step that got skipped because it had never mattered before. You learn to look at a situation and ask what everyone is taking for granted—because that is almost always where it went wrong.

I do software now. I write code for a living, and I spend my own hours trying to break the kind of code I write. It turns out that habit of hunting the quiet failure transfers almost perfectly. Security bugs that make noise get caught: the crash, the stack trace, the scanner alert. The ones that survive to production are the ones that look completely fine.

Which is how I ended up fixated on broken access control.

Why authorization, specifically

An authorization flaw doesn't crash anything. The endpoint returns 200. The JSON is well-formed. Response times are normal, logs are clean, and every automated test passes. The only thing wrong is who got the data—and no scanner on earth knows what your access rules were supposed to be, because you never wrote them down anywhere a machine could read.

That gap is why it stays the most common serious finding in real assessments, and why it is almost always found by a human being patiently trying one more request as one more user. It is tedious, it does not automate well, and it is exactly the sort of problem worth building tools for.

So I did. VardrGate makes you state your access rules explicitly and then tests them on every commit. VardrMap exists because I got tired of not being able to answer "what have I actually looked at?" VardrRunner keeps scanning on the operator's own machine, where it belongs. Each one started as a thing I needed and could not find.

Being an engineer is the point

I'm not a consultant who learned to read code. I ship software, review pull requests, and live with the consequences of my own architectural decisions. When I hand you a finding, it comes with a fix that accounts for your deadline and your existing patterns—because I know exactly what it feels like to receive a report that says "implement centralized authorization" two weeks before a release.

Authorization is not negotiable

Every piece of testing happens with explicit written permission and a documented scope. Nothing outside that scope gets touched, ever. This is not a legal disclaimer—it's why VardrForge bakes target allowlists into the tools it generates, so a script physically cannot be pointed somewhere it was not authorised to go. I'd rather the guardrail live in the code than in my memory.

Findings you can act on

If I report something critical, I can reproduce it in front of you. If I can't reproduce it, I don't report it. No severity inflation to justify an invoice, no padding the finding count, no thirty-page appendix of scanner output nobody reads. A short report where everything is real beats a long one where half of it isn't.

Honest about the limits

I work full time as a software engineer and take a small number of engagements alongside it. That means realistic timelines instead of agency ones, and no pretending to offer emergency incident response. If you have an active breach you need a team that answers the phone at 3am. Telling you otherwise would cost you the one thing you can't get back.

What I'm working toward

The tools are the long game. Every assessment I've done has left me with the same thought—that most of what I just did by hand should have been catchable automatically, if only someone had written the rules down. I want to close that gap: make authorization testing something a team runs on every commit rather than something they buy once a year and hope covers enough.

Alongside that, the checklists on this site are the short version of what I've learned, free and copy-pasteable into your own tickets and code reviews. If they save one team from shipping an IDOR, they've done their job.

Let's talk

Questions about the tools, an authorization design you want a second opinion on, or a review you'd like scoped—I'm interested in all three. If I'm not the right fit, I'll tell you that plainly and point you somewhere better.
